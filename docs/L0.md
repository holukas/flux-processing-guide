title: "L0 · Preliminary run"

[fluxrun](scripts/fluxrun.md) runs EddyPro with relaxed settings. The preliminary fluxes are used for checks and help to find the best settings for the [L1 final flux run](L1.md).

- **Boxes:** 4 and 5 in the [processing chain](index.md).
- **Input:** raw data CSV files from [bico](Raw_Data_Logging.md#conversion) or [rECord](Raw_Data_Logging.md), compressed (`.gz`) or not.
- **Settings:** an EddyPro settings file (`.eddypro`) with a `.metadata` file of the same name next to it.
- **No meteo input:** L0 runs without the 6 meteo variables.
- **Output:** preliminary fluxes.
- **Runs on:** your own computer. It can also run automatically, e.g. once a day.
- **Next step:** [L1 final flux run](L1.md).

## Why L0

- **Not everything is ready during the year:** e.g. the screened meteo data for EddyPro are not available yet.
- **The spectral correction needs a long period:** at least several months, especially Fratini et al. (2012), which also uses RH classes.
- **A test run:** if L0 gives reasonable fluxes with relaxed settings, the data are most likely OK.
- **Settings for L1:** once the year is complete, the L0 results help to set up the L1 run.

## No strict rules

- L0 has no fixed settings. They depend on what you want to check. Try settings and see how they affect the fluxes.
- Some settings may not be final yet, e.g. the vertical separation of the gas analyzer.
- A faster spectral correction saves time: Moncrieff et al. (1997) instead of Fratini et al. (2012) or Horst (1997).
- Minimal output is enough.

See [L0 and L1 compared](L1.md#l0-and-l1-compared) for the differences.

## What the L0 fluxes are used for

- **Check the site:** are the data complete, do the fluxes look plausible?
- **Time lags:** find the nominal lag and the time lag window for the L1 run.
- **Wind direction:** compare the wind direction across years.

## Time lags (OPENLAG)

Knowing the "true" time lag is essential before the fluxes of a year are finalized. The OPENLAG run shows whether a clear lag can be found, and whether it stays in a similar range over the period.

- **When:** before every L1 run, and always when it is not known where the lag lies, e.g. for a new instrument.
- **Time lag compensation:** "Covariance maximization", without a default. EddyPro then writes the lag it really found, with no fallback value.
- **No nominal lag.**
- **Wide window:** typically 0 to 10 s. Windows from -5 to +20 s have also been used. There are no rules.
- **Minimum:** 0 s or negative. With a start value of 0 s, EddyPro started the lag search later, e.g. at +1 s; -1 s avoids that.
- **Maximum:** 10 s or 20 s. A long inlet line of a closed-path analyzer can need more, but a lag of 20 s would be unusual.
- **Narrow down:** if the lags cluster in a smaller range, a second OPENLAG run with a narrower window gives a clearer peak.

### Finding the nominal lag

1. **Check the L0 flux first:** does it look plausible?
2. **Load the lags:** e.g. `co2_time_lag` in the EddyPro `_full_output_` file.
3. **Look at a heatmap:** during daytime, the lag should be found at a similar place.
4. **Plot a histogram with one bin per possible lag:** EddyPro writes lags in steps of 0.05 s, so the number of bins is (maximum lag - minimum lag) / 0.05 + 1, e.g. 201 bins for 0 to 10 s. With fewer or more bins, different lags are pooled into one bin.
5. **Ignore the edge bins:** lags pile up at the window limits when no clear lag was found.
6. **Find the peak bin:** zoom in, with two decimals. The peak is the nominal lag for L1.

- **IRGA (CO2, H2O):** the L1 run uses the peak as nominal lag, with a narrow window around it.
- **QCL and LGR (N2O, CH4):** the L1 run uses the peak as a constant lag, without a window.
- **Different gases:** each gas can have its own lag. The lag of passive gases (CO2, CH4, N2O) does not depend on the weather. The lag of H2O depends strongly on relative humidity, so in closed-path analyzers it is generally longer than that of CO2.
- **Two peaks:** a histogram with two peaks means that the lag changed. Split the data into periods and give each period its own lag.
- **Drifting lags:** with very low fluxes, e.g. N2O and CH4, the lag can drift over time. [dyco](https://github.com/holukas/dyco) detects and removes time lags in the raw data (Vitale et al., 2024).

### Window size

From the EddyPro help:

- **Too narrow:** the actual lag often lies outside the window. EddyPro then often uses the default or a window limit. This leads to systematic flux underestimation.
- **Too wide:** unrealistic lags are found more often, especially when the covariance is small and varies erratically with the lag. This often leads to flux overestimation.

### Automatic time lag optimization

- **What it does:** EddyPro estimates the window and the default lag itself, for H2O in RH classes.
- **Not recommended:** at one site it gave unrealistic CO2 lags. The likely reason: the logger buffers records, e.g. 20 records (1 s), and EddyPro, which estimates the lag from the flow rate, does not know about that buffer.
- **Leaving the nominal lag at 0:** EddyPro then calculates it from the tube length and the flow rate. This gives e.g. about 0.15 s, far below lags found in OPENLAG runs, e.g. 1.25 s. Different lag settings can explain differences between flux datasets.

## Wind direction

- Compare the histogram of the wind direction of each year with a reference period.
- The offset is the number of degrees to add to the wind direction of a year to get the histogram closest to the reference.
- Small offsets across all years mean that the orientation of the sonic anemometer stayed the same.
